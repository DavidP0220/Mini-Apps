# Why Didn't Evolution Remove Your Appendix?

Miniatura: rojo `IT'S NOT USELESS` / blanco `YOUR BODY` / amarillo `IS KEEPING IT` /
barra `AND IT KNOWS WHY`
Objetivo: ~2380 palabras · 15:00 · 225 planos

Tesis: el apendice no es un resto inutil. Es un refugio de bacterias
intestinales que funciona perfecto — y por eso mismo, en un mundo con agua
limpia, se queda sin trabajo, se tapa y mata.

---

## HOOK

There is an organ inside you right now. Your own doctor will tell you it is
useless.

It sits low on the right side of your abdomen. It is the size of your little
finger. You have one. So does everyone you know.

For a hundred and fifty years, medicine has agreed on one thing about it. It
does nothing.

**Your appendix.**

Here is what nobody explains. If it does nothing, it should have shrunk away.
That is what evolution does. It took our tails. It thinned our fur.

But this one is still here. Full size. In all of us.

And it can kill you.

**Why didn't evolution remove your appendix — and nobody has ever explained
why.**

The answer is not that evolution missed it. The answer is that it was never
useless, and the man who called it useless was the most famous biologist who
ever lived.

---

## ACTO 1 · La explicacion facil y su muerte

The standard answer is leftovers.

The story goes like this: our ancestors ate leaves. Leaves are hard to digest,
so they needed a large fermentation chamber in the gut. When our diet changed,
that chamber shrank, and the appendix is what is left of it. A tail end. A
scar from an older menu.

Charles Darwin wrote this himself, in 1871. He listed the appendix as a
vestigial remnant, evidence of our descent from leaf-eating ancestors. Coming
from him, it stopped being a hypothesis and became a fact.

It is also wrong, and the reason is simple enough to check.

Evolution does not politely leave useless organs at full size. It reclaims
them. Building tissue costs energy, and tissue that can become infected costs
lives. A structure with no function gets smaller, generation after generation,
until it is a trace.

That is not what happened here. Your appendix is not a shrivelled remainder.
It has its own blood supply. It has a dedicated muscular wall. And it is
packed with lymphatic tissue — the same tissue your body uses to fight
infection, in concentrations you do not find in a leftover.

Bodies do not spend that on garbage.

Think about what a real vestige looks like, because we have several. The
muscles around your ears are still wired — a few people can wiggle them — but
they are thin, weak and shrinking, and they cost almost nothing. Your tailbone
is three or four fused vertebrae where a tail used to be, and it does not have
its own blood supply or its own muscular wall. That is what abandonment looks
like: smaller, cheaper, on its way out.

The appendix looks like the opposite. It looks maintained.

And there is a second problem with Darwin's version, which is that the animals
do not cooperate. If the appendix were the shrinking remainder of a
leaf-eating gut, it should be largest in the leaf eaters and absent in
everyone else. It is not. Koalas, which eat almost nothing but leaves, have an
enormous fermentation chamber and no appendix at the end of it. Rabbits have
one. Wombats have one. We have one. The pattern does not line up with diet at
all.

When a theory predicts a pattern and the pattern is not there, the theory is
finished. It only survived this long because of whose name was on it.

---

## ACTO 2 · La explicacion moderna y su muerte

The second answer is that appendicitis is a modern problem. Low fibre. Refined
food. Western diets. Fix the diet, fix the appendix.

That one is more interesting, because it is partly true and completely
insufficient.

Appendicitis does vary between populations. But it shows up in societies with
diets that have nothing in common with each other, and it showed up long
before processed food existed. Egyptian mummies show it. Medieval remains show
it.

And here is the detail that kills the diet theory outright: the organ itself is
not degrading in any of those populations. It is not thinner in one and thicker
in another. **It is maintained.** Identically. Everywhere.

A part your body is actively maintaining, at cost, across every human
population on earth, is not a leftover.

And the maintenance is not passive. Your appendix is one of the densest
concentrations of lymphatic tissue anywhere in your digestive system. That
tissue is expensive, it is metabolically active, and it is the same tissue
your body deploys where it expects a fight. You do not garrison an abandoned
building.

So we have an organ that everyone calls useless, that the body pays to keep,
that has no obvious job, and that occasionally kills the person carrying it.

Something in that description has to be wrong. It is the word useless.

---

## ACTO 3 · El mecanismo

In 2007, three researchers at Duke University — Randal Bollinger, William
Parker and their colleagues — published a paper that reframed the whole
question. They were not asking what the appendix used to do. They were asking
what it is doing now.

Their answer was this: your gut is not empty. It holds trillions of bacteria,
and you cannot live without them. They break down what you cannot, they train
your immune system, and they occupy the space that dangerous bacteria would
otherwise take.

That population is your most valuable and most fragile possession. There are
more bacterial cells in your intestine than there are stars you can see with a
telescope, and the specific mix of them took years to assemble. It is not a
generic sludge. It is a particular community, tuned to you, and it cannot be
rebuilt from nothing.

And every so often, something wipes it out. Dysentery. Cholera. Severe
infection. For most of human history, those were not rare events. They were
ordinary. A bad season, bad water, and the entire contents of your intestine
were flushed out in a day.

**Comment one if you have had yours removed. Comment zero if you still have
it.** I want to see the split.

So what refills it?

The appendix is a dead-end tube, narrow, with a single opening, tucked to one
side of the main flow. Anything living inside it is protected from the flood
that empties the rest of the gut. The walls are coated in biofilm — a dense,
organised colony of the exact bacteria you need.

**It is a backup drive.** Not a leftover. A safe house.

Picture it. It is 1348. You drink from a well that everyone drinks from. Two
days later your body empties itself completely. Everyone in your household is
doing the same thing. Half of them will not recover.

You will. And the reason is a small tube you have never heard of, quietly
holding a copy of you.

When the storm passes, the appendix reseeds the intestine with a clean copy of
what was lost. Days instead of months. In a world without clean water, that
difference is the difference between living and not.

And notice how well the shape fits the job. A dead end, so the current does not
run through it. Narrow, so very little gets flushed out. One opening, so it can
close itself off and then reopen. Rich in immune tissue, so the colony inside
stays the right colony and nothing hostile moves in. Positioned at the exact
junction between the small intestine and the large one — the point where a
recolonisation would have to begin.

Every one of those features is a cost. Together they describe a piece of
equipment, not an accident.

That scenario was not exceptional. For almost all of human history, water was
shared with animals and with sewage, and severe intestinal infection was one of
the most common ways to die, especially before the age of five. A mechanism
that shortened recovery from months to days would have been under enormous
selective pressure, generation after generation, for as long as there have been
humans.

Which is exactly what the evidence shows.

---

## ACTO 4 · El giro oscuro

I want to be honest about the part that is uncomfortable.

Once you see the appendix as a backup, the obvious question is why it kills
people. And the answer is not reassuring.

The same design that makes it a good safe house makes it a good trap. A narrow
dead-end tube with one opening is exactly the shape that blocks. When it
blocks, bacteria multiply behind the blockage, pressure builds, the blood
supply is cut off, and the wall dies. Then it bursts, and everything inside it
goes into your abdomen.

Before surgery, that was fatal in most cases. Roughly one person in fifteen
develops appendicitis at some point. Do the arithmetic on a world with no
operating rooms.

And it is worse than a lottery, because of who it takes. Appendicitis peaks in
the teens and twenties — not in the old, not in infants, but precisely in the
people who have already survived childhood and have not yet had children of
their own. Evolution is indifferent to how you die at seventy. It is not
indifferent to how you die at nineteen.

So this organ was killing people at the single worst moment it could, and it
was kept anyway.

So evolution kept an organ that killed a meaningful fraction of every
generation.

That is not a mistake. That is a price. Natural selection does not optimise
your survival — it trades. And for three hundred thousand years, this trade
was worth taking: a small chance of dying at twenty against a much larger
chance of dying at eight from an infection your gut could not recover from.

The appendix was never protecting you from appendicitis. It was protecting you
from something worse.

---

## ACTO 5 · El giro meta

And here's where it flips.

The question everyone asks is why evolution kept a useless organ. That
question has no answer, because the premise is false.

The real question is much stranger. **Why did evolution keep a useful organ
that kills you?**

And once you ask it that way, the appendix stops being a curiosity and becomes
the clearest example of a rule that governs your entire body: nothing in you
was designed to be safe. Everything in you was priced.

Heather Smith and her colleagues followed this to its conclusion by looking
outside our species. If the appendix were a vestige, it should appear in a few
related animals and be lost over and over. The opposite is true. It has
appeared independently in mammals dozens of separate times — in rabbits, in
wombats, in primates — and once a lineage has it, it almost never loses it.

You do not evolve the same structure thirty times by accident. That is the
signature of something being selected for, over and over, in lineages that
share nothing but the same problem.

And it tells you what the problem was. Rabbits, wombats and humans do not share
a diet or a habitat or a body plan. What they share is a gut full of bacteria
they cannot live without, and a world that periodically empties it.

The antagonist in this story is not your appendix. **It is clean water.**

And that is a strange sentence, so sit with it. The single greatest public
health achievement in human history — separating drinking water from sewage —
is the thing that put this organ out of work. Nobody would undo it. Clean water
saved more lives than every surgeon who has ever lived. But it also took a
piece of equipment that had a job for three hundred thousand years and left it
loaded, sealed and idle inside a hundred million people.

That is what progress does. It does not remove the old machinery. It just stops
calling on it.

---

## ACTO 6 · La consecuencia

Here is the mismatch, and it is complete.

The appendix was built for a world where your gut was emptied regularly and
violently. Sewers ended that world. Chlorinated water ended it. In a developed
country you may go your entire life without a single event that requires a
backup.

The organ does not know that. It sits there, full, maintained, waiting for a
catastrophe that is never coming. And an unused narrow tube is a tube with time
to block.

There is even evidence that it still matters. A team at Duke found that
patients without an appendix were roughly four times more likely to suffer
recurring *Clostridium difficile* infections — the exact scenario the appendix
exists for, the gut wiped out and needing to be reseeded.

Which means the organ we call useless is doing its job right up until the
moment it kills you, and doing it for people who will never need it.

**Comment eight if you never knew what your appendix was for.** I read them.

This is the same shape as every other mismatch in your body. The sweet tooth
that was right when sugar was rare. The stress response that was right when the
threat was physical. The appendix that was right when the water was filthy.
None of them are broken. All of them are answering a question the world stopped
asking.

---

## CIERRE

So what do you do with an organ that is working perfectly for a world that no
longer exists?

Nothing. That is the honest answer. You leave it alone, and if it blocks, you
let a surgeon take it out, and you get on with your life.

But the next time someone tells you the human body is badly designed, remember
what is actually sitting in your abdomen. Not a mistake. Not a leftover. A
piece of equipment built for conditions we spent two centuries escaping, still
loaded, still maintained, still waiting.

Darwin was wrong about this one, and it took a hundred and thirty-six years to
find out. That should tell you something about how much of your own body you
have been told is pointless simply because nobody had looked hard enough yet.

Your appendix is not a design flaw.

It is a spare tire for a road we stopped driving on — and you are still
carrying it, in case the road comes back.
